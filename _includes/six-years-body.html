Six years
of AI in Science

Accelerate Science and the changing dynamics of AI adoption

Published 23 November 2026

Much of the discussion about AI in science focuses on technological advances: what new models can do, which scientific problems they might solve, and how quickly their capabilities are improving. As these technologies develop, the communities of researchers engaging with them are also changing, along with the questions they ask and the support they need.

Established in 2020 with funding from Schmidt Sciences, the Accelerate Programme for Scientific Discovery at the University of Cambridge was set up to equip researchers with the tools and skills needed to apply AI to scientific problems. Offering a mix of research funding, education and training, and community engagement, the programme has worked with researchers across disciplines and at different stages of their engagement with AI.

Six years of experience provide an opportunity to examine how that engagement has evolved: who is interested in AI, how researchers develop their capabilities, and how their needs change as the technology advances.

1. AI is reaching a broader research community

Over the past six years, Accelerate has built a growing community of researchers engaging with AI across the University. Growth has accelerated in recent years, reflecting increasing interest in AI technologies and demand for the skills and support needed to use them.

Engagements now span more than 90 departments, illustrating the breadth of interest in AI across Cambridge's research community.

Figure 2. Annual engagements by University School.

The composition of this community has also changed. In the programme's early years, most participants came from the Schools of Biological Sciences, Clinical Medicine, Physical Sciences and Technology. By 2026, engagement had broadened, with growing interest from those Schools alongside researchers in the Humanities and Social Sciences.

These changes offer a perspective on how interest in AI is spreading across disciplines. However, growth has been uneven, with continued strong demand from established technical and scientific communities. This illustrates how widening access to AI is an ongoing process.

2. Researchers take different pathways into AI

Researchers engaged with Accelerate through training courses, AI Clinic support, grant funding, and community events. Some participate in a single activity, while others return repeatedly or move between different forms of support as their interests and projects develop.

The programme has adapted its activities in response to these different needs.

In its early years, engagements were largely through longer, online courses that introduced AI. As interest grew, the programme expanded its offering to include shorter, in-person courses spanning topics from diffusion models to agentic AI, reflecting demand for specialist knowledge and opportunities to acquire new skills quickly. Across the six years, the programme has delivered more than 50 courses, with demand regularly exceeding available places.

Alongside individual support, Accelerate has helped create connections between researchers with shared interests in AI. Community events, including the annual AI for Science Summit, have brought together researchers across disciplines and institutions. Connections made through the programme have also supported the formation of new communities, including a special interest group in AI for protein structure prediction.

Megan Ennion, PhD student, Faculty of Education, University of Cambridge

The community has subsequently contributed to wider research exchange, including an international conference at Cambridge.

Together, these experiences illustrate that researchers do not necessarily follow a single pathway from introductory training to advanced application. Different forms of technical, educational, and community support can complement one another, allowing researchers to engage with AI in ways that reflect their experience and research needs.

3. As AI advances, researchers' questions change

The types of support researchers seek also offer insight into how their engagement with AI is developing.

Alongside training, Accelerate has established an AI Clinic providing tailored technical support from machine learning experts. Available to researchers across the University, the Clinic responds to questions ranging from how to get started with AI to resolving complex issues with advanced code. The number of requests for support from the Clinic has increased over time.

The questions researchers bring to the AI Clinic provide another perspective on changing demand. Earlier enquiries frequently concerned the suitability of AI methods for particular problems. More recent enquiries have included implementation, computational resources, and the development of research proposals. Although the numbers are relatively small, these enquiries illustrate the practical challenges researchers encounter as they move from exploring AI to incorporating it into research workflows, and the importance of institutional support that can respond to changing needs.

These patterns may reflect a shift in the kinds of problems researchers are seeking to address as they gain experience with AI. They also illustrate how technical support services can provide information about emerging research needs and recurring obstacles.

Changes can also be seen in the projects researchers are developing.

Accelerate's seed funding and follow on schemes have supported 60 research projects over the past five years, providing opportunities to explore applications of AI across different disciplines. As the field has evolved, funded projects have increasingly included applied research addressing specific disciplinary problems and taking AI methods towards practical use.

The combination of training demand, Clinic enquiries, and research project proposals offers a view of how researchers' interests and needs are evolving. It can also help identify emerging applications, common technical challenges, and areas where further institutional support may be valuable.

What can we learn?

Six years of Accelerate show how the communities engaging with AI, the opportunities they pursue and the support they seek have changed alongside the technology.

Three lessons emerge from this experience.

1. AI adoption is spreading, but demand develops differently across disciplines.

Changes in the disciplinary composition of engagement illustrate how interest in AI is extending across the research system, creating new demands for expertise and support. Institutions need to understand where this demand is emerging. Adoption support developed for early adopters will not necessarily meet the needs of every research community.

2. Adoption requires different forms of support, which need to evolve with researchers' needs.

There is no single route from initial interest in AI to its application in research. Researchers draw on training, technical expertise, funding, and communities in different combinations, and their questions change as technologies and research ambitions develop. Sustained programmes can adapt their support in response to these changes.

3. Supporting adoption is also an opportunity to learn about the future of AI in science.

Adoption initiatives provide signals about emerging scientific opportunities, the limitations researchers encounter, and the capabilities they need. These insights can inform decisions about institutional support, future research priorities, and investment in AI infrastructure.

Accelerate's experience offers a perspective on how research institutions can respond to a rapidly changing technological field. As AI capabilities continue to develop, the ability to learn from researchers' experiences is key to the next stages of AI for science deployment.
